# What the eventual pitch must prove

**October 24, 2026.** This is a requirements/evidence checklist for a later deck, not the deck itself. No property owner, city department or prospective team member has approved the concept. A positive conversation is not a venue license.

## Frame the decision for the actual audience

The owner is being asked to allow unusual public use of an asset during a major redevelopment. The decision is therefore about **incremental benefit, execution risk, construction protection and accountable cash**, as well as art. Management must be able to send a short package to property operations, finance, legal, its creative team and, where applicable, lenders and public-agency counterparts without rewriting the premise.

Lead with one clear sentence about the event and the conditional venue request; immediately show why it serves this rental/mixed-use project. Put an honest owner-return comparison early. Then establish the audience, people, financing and site controls that make those returns possible. An unsupported large art-sales number on page one would weaken credibility.

Sequoia's published framework usefully separates purpose, problem, solution, market, business model, team and financials. The adaptation below is specific to a landlord's event decision; it is not a claim that a venture-capital template governs real-estate licensing. [Communication framework](https://articles.sequoiacap.com/writing-a-business-plan)

## Recommended contents of the later owner deck

Aim for approximately **12–15 concise slides plus a diligence appendix**. That is a document-length recommendation, not an event-duration recommendation. The deck should be readable without a live narrator. Source every historical number and visibly label assumptions.

| Decision topic | What management needs to understand | Evidence to prepare, not invent |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Concept and decision requested | What it is, why this location, and exactly what management is being asked to authorize next | Defined next-stage consent; no implication that the fair itself is already approved |
| 2. Fit with redevelopment | How cultural activity serves rentals, commercial leasing, neighborhood identity and the owner's creative direction | Verified project facts, owner objectives, measurable benefit categories |
| 3. Site and construction window | Which address, floors and concourse zones could be used without harming redevelopment | Owner-approved footprint and possession/phasing plan; operational dependencies |
| 4. Collector proposition | Why a serious buyer would travel or transact here | Curatorial thesis, relevant work/price bands, credible galleries and buyer research |
| 5. Public proposition | Why regular residents, artists and younger visitors would feel welcome | Free/public-access mechanism, accessible experience, paid local creative participation |
| 6. Market evidence | What indicates actual demand, not just social attention | Qualified collector interviews, appropriately consented pipeline, conditional exhibitor interest; separate existing-event results from targets |
| 7. Sales and revenue bridge | How completed artwork value becomes organizer income, if at all | Seller terms, fee assumptions, sponsorship, ticketing, exclusions, recognition method |
| 8. Owner economics | What the owner receives in weak, middle and strong cases, and who bears costs/losses | Share-basis comparison, expense/service schedule, downside and cash waterfall |
| 9. Team and governance | Who has delivered comparable work and who can stop an unsafe/underfunded show | Accepted roles, verified credits, availability, references, decision rights; gaps labeled vacant |
| 10. Funded delivery | How people, artists, permits and production are paid before opening | Quotes, funding evidence, payment schedule, cash reserves and cancellation liabilities |
| 11. City and neighborhood readiness | Why the event supports public goals without avoidable disruption or approval surprises | Permit/occupancy path, construction interface, access/egress, community coordination, noise and public-benefit measures |
| 12. Partnership integration | How the owner's existing creative operation and surrounding institutions fit | Agreed interfaces; proposed collaborators labeled prospective; no unauthorized logos |
| 13. Risk and fallback | What happens if construction, finance or collector demand disappoint | Resize/relocate/cancel gates; refund plan; no reliance on future seller proceeds |
| 14. Next decision | Who approves what, on what evidence, before further exposure | Written scope for diligence and internal routing; later separate venue/funding approvals |

Useful appendices: source/assumption register, permitted footprint drawings, budget/cash schedule, proposed deal definitions, insurance indication, producer credits, artist/seller agreement outline, collector research method and a redacted pipeline summary. Private personal information belongs in controlled diligence materials, not the public repository.

## What “do not upset the city” should mean in practice

Treat it as a requirement for early coordination, clear responsibility and genuine public value. It is not evidence that officials oppose the event, nor an instruction to avoid oversight. Do not imply city endorsement, use municipal branding, announce approved attendance or promise public money before confirmation.

Denver distinguishes the Office of Special Events process for public-property activity from private indoor events. Being outside that particular process does **not** settle building use, fire approval, accessibility, alcohol, noise, tax, food or right-of-way requirements. A publicly marketed event inside a privately owned office building is not exempt from those other obligations simply because entry is by ticket or invitation. [City scope reference](https://denvergov.org/files/assets/public/v/1/city-council/documents/d5/new-folder/city_council_reference_event_sponsorships.pdf)

| Issue to resolve with qualified local people | Owner/city-facing evidence | Why it affects the concept |
|---|---|---|
| Approved use and public assembly | Existing occupancy records, proposed use, code analysis and required approvals for each zone | Office vacancy is not permission to operate a large assembly venue |
| Occupant load and evacuation | Floor-by-floor plan, exit/stair capacity, emergency access, staffed evacuation and elevator contingencies | Whole-building area does not determine safe attendance; upper floors create queue/evacuation constraints |
| Construction and existing tenants | Separated routes/zones, fire-system continuity, work/noise schedule and contractor/property-manager approval | Conversion activity or an office tenant may take priority over event access |
| Accessibility | Step-free routes, toilets, viewing/communication provisions, seated rest, accessible ticketing and emergency plan | “Accessible art” must include disabled visitors and workers, not just affordable admission |
| Installation engineering | Floor/point loads, rigging, power, heat, projection, temporary walls, materials and effects | A spectacular work may be impossible or uneconomic on an approved floor |
| Public realm | Ownership/easements, queuing, loading, street/sidewalk impacts, RTD coordination as applicable | A private concourse or plaza may still interface with regulated public space |
| Neighbors and environment | Sound/hours, transport, waste, food service, incident contact and restoration | Avoid preventable complaints and visible neglect |
| Financial/public claims | Separate event income, seller balances and any restricted public support | Public money must not be presented as unrestricted founder or owner profit |
| Alcohol and commercial activity | Correct licensee/permit route, seller tax responsibilities and licensed vendors where required | Do not assume a commercial fair can simply obtain a nonprofit special-event liquor license |
| Insurance and incident response | Event/general liability, art custody and transit, workers coverage, medical/security plan and cancellation exposure | General liability alone does not insure every artwork or construction loss |

The city's fire guidance specifically covers fairs/exhibits, assembly activity and certain production effects. Its quick-permit page includes special events no longer than three days **within its electrical quick-permit categories**; that is not a universal event-length limit or general event/occupancy approval. A longer or more complex activation needs the applicable review path established for its actual use. Do not choose a nominal three-day format merely to assume approvals disappear. [Denver-authored fire guidance](https://denver.prelive.opencities.com/files/assets/public/v/2/fire-department/documents/dfd-special-events-guidelines-2024.pdf), [current quick-permit categories](https://www.denvergov.org/Government/Agencies-Departments-Offices/Agencies-Departments-Offices-Directory/Community-Planning-and-Development/Plan-Review-Permits-and-Inspections/Quick-Permits)

Denver's published multi-agency event timeline includes 60-, 45- and 30-day milestones for different activities and a final pre-setup completion requirement. These are procedural minima for applicable processes, not a sensible allowance for first assessing a multi-floor conversion site. The later plan should work backward from written agency and owner requirements, with extra time for redesign. [City timeline](https://www.denvergov.org/files/assets/public/v/1/special-events/documents/event-application-timeline.pdf)

## What gives the event art-market legitimacy?

Art Basel's published application process uses gallery selection committees. The transferable lesson is independent, credible selection and clear standards—not copying a name, an admission policy or an assumed sales total. [Art Basel gallery applications](https://www.artbasel.com/about/application)

For this project, distinguish **the art market**, **public cultural programming** and **sponsor hospitality** within one coherent experience. Buyers need quality, provenance, knowledgeable staff, reliable fulfillment and enough quiet/time to choose work. Public audiences need welcome, fair access and excellent programming. Sponsors need specific deliverables. Loud evening spectacle should not make daytime art impossible to view or buy.

Proposed standards to evaluate later:

- Independent selection with disclosed commercial conflicts; no guarantee of inclusion because a gallery pays a sponsor fee.
- Clear seller/artist authority, artwork information and pricing; qualified handling, condition reports and shipment/installation support.
- A small collector-services function that schedules meaningful visits and follows through on requests; no invented buyer list or implied museum acquisitions.
- Strong photography, color integrity, typography, wayfinding, accessible digital pages and reliable checkout; assets licensed before public reuse.
- Artist pay and settlement measures alongside attendance, including completed net seller receipts and the distribution of outcomes. A headline GMV figure can hide most artists selling nothing.

## Readiness gates before the actual deck makes claims

| Gate | Evidence needed | Present status |
|---|---|---|
| Site plausibility | Owner-approved window/footprint; construction separation; code consultant's scoped assessment | Missing |
| Delivery credibility | Experienced executive producer and finance/collector leads with accepted scopes | Missing |
| Paid discovery | Funds/contracts sufficient for defined feasibility work without speculative unpaid hiring | Missing |
| Commercial plausibility | Conditional exhibitor/collector/sponsor evidence and a cost/cash model informed by quotes | Not established; current scenarios are illustrations |
| External communication | Approved wording, rights, responsible legal entity and clear scope of any permission | Missing |

The next presentation can be an honest request to investigate a bounded concept. It should not be dressed as an already-financed, city-approved event. Once a later deck is authorized, tailor the ask to the actual internal approver; the intended recipient and routing authority remain unknown.
