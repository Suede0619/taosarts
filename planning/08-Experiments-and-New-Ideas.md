# Experiments that could make Taos distinctive

These are new proposals for this project, not claims that nobody in the world has ever imagined them. Their value depends on artist consent, buyer demand, execution, and evidence. Do not commit to all of them at once. Experiment caps below are proposed incremental test budgets, not additions already funded in the operating model.

## 1. A buyer brief exchange, rather than an endless catalog

A buyer gives a real budget, size, interests, deadline, and permission to circulate an anonymized brief. Eligible artists and galleries respond with **existing suitable works**, not free speculative concepts. A human editor returns a short selection. This brings documented demand to artists rather than asking every artist to compete for anonymous attention.

Test: ten genuine briefs, maximum $1,000 incremental coordination cost. Success: at least three paid purchases, useful seller feedback, and no representation disputes. Stop if briefs are vague, the buyer has no authority, or responding requires unpaid custom labor. Proposed owner: collector lead, first quarter.

## 2. Students and residents become purchasing councils

A donor funds a small acquisition budget for a willing school, library, clinic, or community venue. Participants learn to discuss and choose real art; artists are paid at agreed prices. The receiving institution approves display, maintenance, and ownership. Publish the decision process rather than treating it as a photo opportunity.

Test: one willing institution and a $5,000 donor-funded purchase budget, plus separately funded facilitation/access. Success: an accepted acquisition, paid artist, meaningful participant learning. CherryArts provides a relevant precedent; this is a local adaptation, not an invented first. [Education program](https://cherryarts.org/education/). Do not assume New Mexico Arts will fund acquisition-focused work.

## 3. “One wall, three possible futures”

With consent, a buyer supplies a private room photo and dimensions. The concierge presents three real available works with scaled visualizations, context, full cost, and installation options. Identify visualization limitations and never alter the work's image misleadingly. Delete room images on the agreed schedule.

Test: ten buyer sessions, $1,000 incremental cap. Success: better qualified viewings or purchases than ordinary shortlists, with buyer understanding of scale. Stop if image rights, privacy, color accuracy, or inventory make the service misleading. No image assets are generated by this document plan.

## 4. Small hosted salons that move toward the buyer

A willing host in Denver or another tested feeder market convenes a small group around a tightly chosen Taos presentation. Use remote artist conversation where travel is wasteful; ship only what makes sense. Make a real purchase path and Taos visit invitation part of the experience.

Test: two sessions, $1,500 incremental cap each. Success: paid purchases or a documented profitable follow-on cohort within 90 days. Stop if the event becomes expensive hospitality without qualified demand. Owner: business/collector lead.

## 5. An availability cooperative

Participating sellers keep control of their relationships but share a minimal, permissioned availability ledger. It solves a basic problem: the same work cannot be promised to two buyers. Each seller sees only the data needed for their role; commercial terms are agreed rather than collusively standardized.

Test: five galleries and 100 works using a simple tool; no bespoke platform initially. Success: at least 98% availability accuracy and faster replies. Stop if the administrative burden exceeds value or permissions cannot be managed. Owner: inventory lead.

## 6. A sponsor funds the obstacle, not just the banner

Ask artists which real barriers prevent participation: packing, photography, transport, childcare during a session, accessibility, or a fee. A restricted sponsor fund pays approved costs under transparent rules. Report the number of artists enabled and their actual outcomes.

Test: $5,000 fund under an authorized entity, with simple equitable allocation and privacy. Success: participation that otherwise would not happen, paid artists, low administrative burden. Stop if selection is opaque or support is contingent on promotional compliance. Owner: community lead.

## 7. A collecting passport without a status hierarchy

A public visitor can collect artist stories and process demonstrations through a printed or digital passport. No purchase is required. A collector can save a genuine shortlist and follow artists with consent. A sponsor may fund a public acquisition when a participation threshold is reached, but the sponsor's actual purchase commitment must exist first.

Test: one route, ten participating stops, $1,000 incremental cap. Measure discovery, return intent, meaningful artist conversations, and separately completed sales. Stop if it creates QR activity without engagement. Do not require tracking children or collecting unnecessary identities.

## 8. An art procurement desk for ordinary institutions

Make it easier for clinics, small hotels, restaurants, and workplaces to buy local art with an approved budget. Offer an understandable process, artist-paid development, phased delivery, and maintenance. Connect contemporary and historical context without imposing a generic “Southwest look.”

Test: five budget-holder interviews and one paid brief before substantial build. Success: a funded order with positive contribution and artists treated fairly. Stop if buyers request broad unpaid speculative design. Owner: procurement lead.

## 9. Care, reframing, and re-engagement

A relationship need not end when a work reaches the wall. With permission, provide care information, installation referrals, reframing, artist news, and occasional collection conversations. A trusted service can create repeat purchasing and referrals without manufacturing urgency.

Test: follow ten completed buyers for 90 days, with their communication preferences. Success: satisfaction, resolved issues, useful introductions, or repeat business. Stop if contact is unwanted or the service makes unsupported conservation claims. Use qualified professionals for technical treatment.

## 10. A public livelihood report that changes the incentives

Publish not only total sales but the distribution: percentage of artists who sold, median proceeds, time to payment, first sales, and concentration. Pair numbers with consented artist accounts of what helped. Report which programs generated useful demand and which did not.

Test: a pilot dashboard with aggregated data and small-group privacy protections. Success: artists and funders use it to improve allocation. Stop or redesign if it becomes ranking or shame. This can differentiate the festival through accountability rather than promotional scale.

## 11. A deliberately short Arroyo Seco art day

Develop one locally supported half-day experience around verified appointments, a meal, landscape interpretation where appropriate, and enough travel time. Residents and participating venues shape capacity and hours. Link the trip to available art and a remote follow-up service, not an obligatory shopping circuit.

Test: one small group after permissions, access, transport, weather, and host costs are established. Success: good resident/artist experience and relevant buyer engagement. Stop if visitors are directed to private sites, parking cannot cope, or the route burdens the community. This is a pilot concept, not an announced itinerary.

## Choose a portfolio, not a wish list

First quarter priorities: the buyer brief exchange, availability cooperative, and two small collector sessions. Develop one funded public acquisition/learning pilot with a willing institution. Defer the others until someone owns the work and its cost is funded.

Score experiments on expected artist benefit, evidence of demand, cash risk, staff effort, cultural/access implications, and reversibility. A failed small experiment should produce learning and a stop decision. A successful one earns a larger test, not automatic regional expansion.
