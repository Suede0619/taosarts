# Experiments that could make Taos distinctive

These project proposals combine existing approaches. The document makes no claim that they lack precedents. Their value depends on artist consent, buyer demand, execution, and evidence. Do not commit to all of them at once. These caps budget incremental tests. The operating model provides no committed funds for them.

## 1. Buyer briefs matched to available work

A buyer gives a real budget, size, interests, deadline, and permission to circulate an anonymized brief. Eligible artists and galleries respond with **existing suitable works**. Pay separately for any new concept work. A human editor returns a short selection. The exchange gives artists a buyer’s requirements, timing and budget.

Test: ten genuine briefs, maximum $1,000 incremental coordination cost. Success: at least three paid purchases, useful seller feedback, and no representation disputes. Stop if briefs are vague, the buyer has no authority, or responding requires unpaid custom labor. Proposed owner: collector lead, first quarter.

## 2. Students and residents become purchasing councils

A donor funds a small acquisition budget for a willing school, library, clinic, or community venue. Participants learn to discuss and choose real art. Pay artists the agreed prices. The receiving institution approves display, maintenance, and ownership. Publish the selection process and results.

Test: one willing institution and a $5,000 donor-funded purchase budget, plus separately funded facilitation/access. Success: an accepted acquisition, paid artist, meaningful participant learning. CherryArts provides a relevant precedent. This adapts an existing approach for local use. [Education program](https://cherryarts.org/education/). Do not assume New Mexico Arts will fund acquisition-focused work.

## 3. “One wall, three possible futures”

With consent, a buyer supplies a private room photo and dimensions. The concierge presents three real available works with scaled visualizations, context, full cost, and installation options. Identify visualization limitations and never alter the work's image misleadingly. Delete room images on the agreed schedule.

Test: ten buyer sessions, $1,000 incremental cap. Success: better qualified viewings or purchases than ordinary shortlists, with buyer understanding of scale. Stop if image rights, privacy, color accuracy, or inventory make the service misleading. The document plan creates no image assets.

## 4. Small hosted salons that move toward the buyer

A willing host in Denver or another tested feeder market convenes a small group around a tightly chosen Taos presentation. Use remote artist conversation where travel is wasteful. Ship only what makes sense. Make a real purchase path and Taos visit invitation part of the experience.

Test: two sessions, $1,500 incremental cap each. Success: paid purchases or a documented profitable follow-on cohort within 90 days. Stop if the event becomes expensive hospitality without qualified demand. Owner: business/collector lead.

## 5. An availability cooperative

Participating sellers keep control of their relationships but share a minimal, permissioned availability ledger. Keep a current reservation record so the seller promises a work to only one buyer. Each seller sees only the data needed for their role. Agree terms separately with each seller. Do not coordinate competing sellers’ prices.

Test: up to five galleries and sixty works, beginning with a smaller cleared set using a simple tool. No bespoke platform initially. Success: at least 98% availability accuracy and faster replies. Stop if administrative cost exceeds value or the team cannot maintain permissions. Owner: inventory lead.

## 6. Sponsor funding for a specific artist or buyer need

Ask artists which real barriers prevent participation: packing, photography, transport, childcare during a session, accessibility, or a fee. A restricted sponsor fund pays approved costs under transparent rules. Report the number of artists enabled and their actual outcomes.

Test: $5,000 fund under an authorized entity, with simple equitable allocation and privacy. Success: participation that otherwise would not happen, paid artists, low administrative burden. Stop if selection is opaque or support is contingent on promotional compliance. Owner: community lead.

## 7. A collecting passport without a status hierarchy

A public visitor can collect artist stories and process demonstrations through a printed or digital passport. Visitors can participate without buying. A collector can save a genuine shortlist and follow artists with consent. Secure the sponsor’s purchase commitment before linking a public acquisition to a participation threshold.

Test: one route, ten participating stops, $1,000 incremental cap. Measure discovery, return intent, meaningful artist conversations, and separately completed sales. Stop if it creates QR activity without engagement. Do not require tracking children or collecting unnecessary identities.

## 8. An art procurement desk for ordinary institutions

Make it easier for clinics, small hotels, restaurants, and workplaces to buy local art with an approved budget. Offer an understandable process, artist-paid development, phased delivery, and maintenance. Connect contemporary and historical context without imposing a generic “Southwest look.”

Test: five budget-holder interviews and one paid brief before substantial build. Success: a funded order with positive contribution and artists treated fairly. Stop if buyers request broad unpaid speculative design. Owner: procurement lead.

## 9. Care, reframing, and re-engagement

A relationship need not end when a work reaches the wall. With permission, provide care information, installation referrals, reframing, artist news, and occasional collection conversations. A trusted service can create repeat purchasing and referrals without manufacturing urgency.

Test: follow ten completed buyers for 90 days, with their communication preferences. Success: satisfaction, resolved issues, useful introductions, or repeat business. Stop unwanted contact and remove unsupported conservation claims. Use qualified professionals for technical treatment.

## 10. A public livelihood report that changes the incentives

Publish total sales, the percentage of artists who sold, median proceeds, time to payment, first sales and sales concentration. Pair numbers with consented artist accounts of what helped. Report which programs generated useful demand and which did not.

Test: a pilot dashboard with aggregated data and small-group privacy protections. Success: artists and funders use it to improve allocation. Stop or redesign if it becomes ranking or shame. Publish the results so artists and buyers can assess performance.

## 11. A deliberately short Arroyo Seco art day

Develop one locally supported half-day experience around verified appointments, a meal, landscape interpretation where appropriate, and enough travel time. Residents and participating venues shape capacity and hours. Offer available art and remote follow-up as optional parts of the trip.

Test with one small group after confirming permissions, access, transport, weather and host costs. Success: good resident/artist experience and relevant buyer engagement. Stop if the route sends visitors to private sites, exceeds parking capacity or burdens residents. The itinerary needs testing before announcement.

## Experiment selection and budget

First quarter priorities: the buyer brief exchange, availability cooperative, and two small collector sessions. Develop one funded public acquisition/learning pilot with a willing institution. Defer other tests until a responsible person and funds cover the work.

Score experiments on expected artist benefit, evidence of demand, cash risk, staff effort, cultural/access implications, and reversibility. A failed small experiment should produce learning and a stop decision. A successful pilot supports a larger test. Regional expansion needs a separate decision.
